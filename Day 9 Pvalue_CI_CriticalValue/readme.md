# 📘 Hypothesis Testing – p-Value, Confidence Interval & Errors
---

## Q.1 Define p-value in your own words and explain its basic interpretation

The **p-value** is the probability of obtaining the observed sample result, or a result more extreme, assuming that the null hypothesis is true. It helps us measure how compatible the sample data is with the null hypothesis.

In interpretation, a **small p-value** indicates that the observed data is unlikely to have occurred purely by chance under the null hypothesis, whereas a **large p-value** suggests that the observed result is consistent with the null hypothesis.

---

## Q.2 Why is a small p-value considered evidence against the null hypothesis?

A small p-value means that the probability of observing such data when the null hypothesis is true is very low. This low probability suggests that the assumption made by the null hypothesis may not be correct.

Therefore, a small p-value provides strong statistical evidence against the null hypothesis and supports the alternative hypothesis.

---

## Q.3 Explain how a p-value helps in making decisions during hypothesis testing

In hypothesis testing, the p-value is compared with a pre-decided significance level (α). This comparison guides the decision-making process.

If the p-value is **less than or equal to α**, the null hypothesis is rejected. If the p-value is **greater than α**, the null hypothesis is not rejected. Thus, the p-value provides an objective rule for accepting or rejecting claims.

---

## Q.4 What is a confidence interval? Explain its purpose in statistics

A **confidence interval** is a range of values calculated from sample data that is likely to contain the true population parameter.

Its purpose is to provide an estimate of the population value along with a measure of uncertainty, rather than giving a single fixed estimate.

---

## Q.5 Give one example showing how a confidence interval is used in real-life decision-making

Suppose a company estimates that the average delivery time for its orders is between **2 and 3 days with 95% confidence**. This interval helps management decide whether delivery performance meets customer expectations and service standards.

---

## Q.6 What is meant by the term "critical value" in hypothesis testing?

A **critical value** is a threshold value obtained from a probability distribution that determines the boundary between the acceptance region and the rejection region of a hypothesis test.

It helps decide whether the test statistic is extreme enough to reject the null hypothesis.

---

## Q.7 How is the critical value related to the significance level (α)?

The critical value is directly determined by the chosen significance level (α). A smaller significance level results in a more extreme critical value.

This relationship ensures that the probability of committing a Type I error does not exceed the chosen α.

---

## Q.8 Explain how the rejection region is determined using a critical value

The rejection region consists of all values of the test statistic that lie beyond the critical value.

If the calculated test statistic falls into this region, the null hypothesis is rejected because the observed result is considered statistically significant.

---

## Q.9 Describe the difference between one-tailed and two-tailed critical regions

In a **one-tailed test**, the rejection region is located entirely on one side of the probability distribution.

In a **two-tailed test**, the rejection regions are divided equally between both ends of the distribution, allowing detection of deviations in either direction.

---

## Q.10 Give an example showing how a critical value helps in drawing conclusions

If the critical value for a two-tailed test at α = 0.05 is ±1.96 and the calculated test statistic is 2.5, the statistic falls beyond the critical value.

This leads to rejection of the null hypothesis, indicating a statistically significant result.

---

## Q.11 Define Type I Error and explain what it means in practical terms

A **Type I Error** occurs when the null hypothesis is rejected even though it is actually true.

In practical terms, it represents a false alarm, such as concluding that a medicine is unsafe when it is actually safe.

---

## Q.12 Define Type II Error and give a simple real-world example

A **Type II Error** occurs when the null hypothesis is not rejected even though it is false.

For example, approving a defective product because the testing process failed to detect the defect.

---

## Q.13 How does reducing the significance level (α) affect Type I and Type II errors?

Reducing the significance level lowers the probability of committing a Type I error.

However, this also increases the probability of committing a Type II error because the criteria for rejection become more strict.

---

## Q.14 Why is it not possible to minimize both Type I and Type II errors at the same time?

There is an inverse relationship between Type I and Type II errors.

Reducing one error increases the likelihood of the other, making it impossible to minimize both simultaneously without increasing sample size.

---

## Q.15 Explain why understanding both error types is important in hypothesis testing

Understanding both Type I and Type II errors helps researchers evaluate risks and consequences of incorrect decisions.

This understanding ensures balanced decision-making, especially in critical fields like medicine, engineering, and business.
